Speed was essential to the Germans plan
when they launched their Ardan offensive
in December 1944.
Success would depend on the fast capture
of the small Belgian town of St. V.
Six major roads converged there, making
the town of just 2,000 residents vital
to both road and rail transportation
connecting Belgium and Germany.
Open roads and rail lines were critical
to supplies like food, fuel, and
ammunition as the Germans sprinted
westward to capture Antworp, the most
important allied supply port in Western
Europe.
Seizing Antworp would significantly
threaten the Allies capability to
continue fighting. Hitler hoped this
would force his Western enemies to
negotiate peace.
Fighting around St. Vith began on the
Battle of the Bulges first day as German
armor and infantry tried to push Allied
resistance aside quickly and bolt toward
Antworp.
At first, the 106th and 28th Infantry
Divisions defended St. Vith opposing the
attack of the German fifth and sixth
panzer armies. They were later joined by
elements of the seventh armored division
to defend St. V and by time for
additional Allied reinforcements to move
forward.
Although the American defenders of St. V
were inexperienced, outnumbered, and
outgunned, they put up stiffer
resistance than the Germans expected.
Rather than taking St. V easily and
pushing beyond it by late afternoon on
December 17th as planned, the Germans
spent days fighting around the town.
The brutal fighting and bitter cold took
its toll on the Americans. On December
21st, Major General Alan Jones,
commander of the decimated 106th
Infantry Division, turned over command
of the St. Va to Brigadier General Bruce
C. Clark, leader of the seventh armored
division's combat command B, a task
force that included tanks, infantry, and
artillery.
Upon relinquishing control of the area,
the demoralized Jones told Clark, "I've
thrown in my last chips."
Clark, who enlisted in the army in 1917
and retired as a four-star general in
1962,
later recalled St. Vith as a 7-day
battle in which I had no time to lay
down or go to sleep and lamented that
terrible winter weather prevented any
Allied air support.
Clark's force alone lost 2,000 men in
the fighting, but their resistance
forced the Germans to commit more time,
troops, and equipment than they could
spare.
Allied forces eventually withdrew from
St. V to avoid being surrounded. But by
the time the Germans finally captured
the town, the fighting there had turned
into a strategic disaster for Hitler's
forces. When their tanks rolled into St.
V 2 days before Christmas, they were
already 6 days behind schedule.
Several German commanders later noted
that it was around St. V that their plan
for victory in the Ardan began to
crumble.
Years after his retirement, General
Clark said that in his long and
distinguished career, I think the most
important action I took part in was the
Battle of St. V during the Battle of the
Bulge. At the end of seven days, the
high water mark of the German army was
reached and we from then on just went
and finished up the war.

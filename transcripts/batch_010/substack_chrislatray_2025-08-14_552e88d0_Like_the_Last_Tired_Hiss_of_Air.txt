Boozhoo, indinawemaaganidog! Aaniin! That is to say hello, all of my relatives! Welcome to another edition of
An Irritable Métis.
Today is an important date in the Annals of Irritability
1
and rather than blather on in this introduction as I often do, I’m just going to get right to it….
One of many articles written during my term, this one courtesy of the
Billings Gazette
regarding my work in Yellowstone, which you may check out
HERE
if you like.
On this date in 2018 my first book,
One-Sentence Journal
, was released and almost immediately began to redirect the course of my writing life, or at least the course I thought it would take. I’m glad I didn’t resist the direction it chose for me, like water finding a way, because it has been a wonderful experience. Fast forward two years from that particular pre-Covid Tuesday to this date in 2023, when I got
the call that informed me
I was being appointed Montana state poet laureate. And now today, on this anniversary, two years complete though to my knowledge a successor hasn’t been announced, I feel that my term is completely finished, put-a-fork-in-it done, kaput. I kinda accepted it as over when I walked off the stage of the Montana Theater on the campus of the University of Montana on Friday, August 1st, with powerhouse comrade poets Sasha taqʷšəblu LaPointe, Denise Lajimodiere, and Joy Harjo, because no way was my term ever going to reach those heights again with such transcendent spirits.
2
Now, like the last tired hiss of air out of a roadworn and balding old tire, it really does feel over.
3
The other night, while doing the first official event related to the paperback release of
Becoming Little Shell
, during the Q&A portion of the event a colleague in the audience – who also happened to be the only other Native person, at least willing to raise their hand when asked, in a standing room only crowd
4
– asked the following question. “If you had another year, or another term, as poet laureate, would you try harder to engage with Native communities?”
I have to tell you it stung. For one thing, she has no way of knowing how hard I tried, as that was a goal of my two-year effort. I did make some inroads; during my term I was on the Fort Peck reservation, Rocky Boy’s a couple times, Fort Belknap a couple times, the Blackfeet reservation a couple times … but failed to make it to the CSKT reservation
5
, the Crow reservation, or the Northern Cheyenne reservation. At least not in any “official” capacity; I’ve put my feet on all of those lands during these last couple years. But ultimately, yes, it largely feels I’ve failed to really create the necessary relationships.
I’d already been thinking about that failure in a piece I wrote for the Montana Arts Council’s quarterly newspaper. I don’t know if it’s out yet or if it was even included and I’m actually too chicken to go looking for it, but I thought I’d include it here as well.
An Overwhelming, Transcendent Celebration of Relationship
by Chris La Tray
If time isn’t a construct, or a figment of our collective imagination, then why can an odyssey around Missoula to merely attend to two or three errands seem, what with all the traffic, interminable, and yet two years in service to the arts community of Montana can fly by in the blink of an eye? Because that’s how it feels sitting here at my desk, a little maudlin, attempting to write this final episode for
State of the Arts
as my tenure as Montana’s 11th poet laureate comes to a breathless and wheezing conclusion.
I received the news of my appointment in the late morning of August 14th, 2023. My phone rang from a Bozeman number and at the time I didn’t think there was anyone from Bozeman I wanted to talk to so I let it go to voice mail. The message was Governor Greg Gianforte sharing the news, and asking me to call him back at that number, his cell phone. Which I did. It might have been the shortest phone call in history between two guys prone to bloviation but I was still pleased to have been granted the opportunity to unleash myself on the state of Montana. Which I’ve made my best effort at doing ever since; I bought a car in May of 2024 that had 25 miles on the odometer when I rolled it off the lot and I just broke 55,000, which accounts roughly for a little over only half of my term’s worth of ambassadoring.
That I was ever even nominated for the position at all begins with a practice I started many years ago of writing one sentence a day, a practice anyone can do that I’ve constantly encouraged folks to try. It’s an enlightening way to not just pay attention to the world, but also the life you are living while engaged with it. That is the essence of poetry, and a practice, sometimes the only one I’m able to maintain a relationship with, that I have continued throughout.
In 2018 I distilled my version of that essence into a collection of poems based on my favorites of those first sentences. That book, my first,
One-Sentence Journal
, came out on August 14, 2018. Five years to the day later is when Governor Gianforte called me, representing a not-insignificant example of coincidence sometimes being more than coincidence. So in honoring those short poems I am going to be brief in my expression of gratitude for what traveling all over the state, speaking to thousands of people, has meant to me. I’ll boil it down to one sentence, in fact, which I recorded in my journal the night almost 300 people showed up at the Missoula Public Library – the Peoples’ House! – to celebrate my poet laureateship:
2023_1010: An overwhelming, transcendent celebration of relationship.
In closing, I also have to take a moment to express some disappointments. When it comes to the goals I set for myself as poet laureate, while I made it to many communities that aren’t often served, I pretty much failed in my efforts to create many relationships in tribal communities. That bums me out, and that effort will continue even as I return to just being a simple rube with no fancy title to cling to. I’m also bummed to see the explosion of AI in schools. I’m absolutely certain that there is nothing more detrimental to the future of education in this country than this technology being rammed down our throats. We should be fighting it every step of the way and we simply aren’t. That has to change. Nothing less than the future of our human relationship to creativity depends on it.
Which leads me to the last thing: The destruction by the federal government of a broad swathe of the public commons as represented by arts organizations is a disaster whose ramifications are yet to be fully determined. It touches all of us and hinders the pursuit of deeper relationships within our broad expanse of communities. That an unelected imbecile with no greater resumé to boast than that he was born smug into wealth and privilege was allowed to gut programs that reach into every single community regardless of how it’s been gerrymandered will go down in history as an unfathomable crime against the greater good. It’s unconscionable behavior that people on both sides of the voting ballot must recognize is a terrible way to run a country. Our elected officials need to recognize that they represent everyone in Montana and show a little political backbone in standing up to these ignorant bullies.
So I’m going to give a big war-whoop of a shout-out to all my comrades at Humanities Montana and the Montana Arts Council, who have all been the most wonderful and supportive gang of mad geniuses I’ve ever been associated with. What a mighty crew constantly standing firm against the tide of ruthless cowards terrified at the power of a motivated and active art community! I’ve been inspired and uplifted by every one of you every moment of these last two years. Now the battle is just getting started and if I know one thing, it is the relentless unstoppability of a committed long game, and we are all engaged in that together.
We’re going to win this thing, my friends.
As a final post as acting poet laureate, I think that piece sums everything up pretty well. The work continues and I’ll continue to be part of it. It’s just getting a lot harder and there’s almost no money from traditional sources
6
to make traveling all over as feasible and I’m going to keep up the effort as long as I can make it work, and so far my calendar remains full. Your support is a big part of that. So many of you have been along every step of the way, and so kind and generous, and I hope we can continue fighting the good fight together. And who knows, perhaps at some point I’ll find the time to actually write something again that isn’t just another ill-thought, meandering newsletter.

3
I purposely use this metaphor because the car I started driving in May of 2024, which had 25 miles on it when I rolled off the lot, has over 58K now. And yes, I’ve driven through one set of tires already. The car doesn’t seem as tired as I am, though.
4
That sounds grandious and, while true, it was a small room at Country Bookshelf in Bozeman, who did a wonderful job with the event.
5
Does my time there prior to being poet laureate working for the
Missoula Writing Collaborative
count? I don’t know. In all my years of spending time there – I call the Flathead Reservation my “step” reservation because the LST doesn’t have one – I really only have one contact with the tribe. Gratitude to you, Michelle, if you read this.
6
I’ve done some rough math and the cuts to Humanities alone will probably be a cut in 20% of my income as well, not even counting the associated expenses associate with keeping myself on the road, which they also (mostly) covered. Maybe it’s time to start hosting online workshops of my own again, I don’t know. Face-to-face is so much better.

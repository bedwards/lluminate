As of this January, Bentham’s Bulldog will be four years old (the publication, not the author).  Woo!  Happy new year to everyone, even the
haters and losers
who have grown increasingly desperate as we have saved shrimp at a level that no one has ever seen before.
At the end of year last year, I had around 4,200 blog readers.  Now I have 9903.  So if doubling trends continue, in 2095, I will have around 1.1805916 x 10^21 blog subscribers.  This strikes me as a conservative estimate, because such things tend to speed up.
Speaking of trends continuing or maybe speeding up, one of my major ideological changes this year is I’ve become convinced that
AI will be a huge deal
.  Specifically, I now think it’s reasonably likely that some time in the next decade or two, we’ll see growth rates much faster than any that have previously been seen in human history.
The other related shift is that I’ve become increasingly convinced of Longtermism.  I’ll write more about this soon, but for a while I accepted Longtermism intellectually but did not really act on it.  For instance, I mostly gave my money to charities that helped animals.  I’ve now become convinced that Longtermist charities are better, and I give them a decent slice of my money.
I’ve also gotten more convinced that
insect welfare is hugely important
.  After learning that the average human prevents about 14 million insect life years per year they’re alive, and that you likely affect more animals by giving to charities saving people than the shrimp welfare project, I’ve started thinking that saving human lives is probably the best short-term charitable endeavor.  I give a significant chunk of my donations now to charities that raise money for effective global health charities.
I wanted to shout out some blogs that I’ve enjoyed this year (this won’t be anything like exhaustive, and I’ll leave out some ones you probably already know).  I loved
Andy Masley
’s
blog
.  Andy is just so smart, meticulous, and careful.  I also met him in person and he was as nice as you’d expect.  One of the things I’ve enjoyed this year has been watching him fight with totally unhinged people about AI water use, and consistently very politely destroy them.
Similarly, my friend
Ibrahim Dagher
started a blog.  I particularly enjoyed
this post
where he discusses the importance of engaging with counterarguments on philosophical subjects.  And my friend
Value Locked In
just started a blog yesterday, which I predict will grow steadily more epic.  Check out his
one post
where he argues for veganism.  I specifically encouraged him to start a blog because I thought it would turn out very good, and I don’t do that for many people.  The guy is locked in—both incredibly smart and thoughtful!
Also, my friend
The Droll Scroll
(who is anonymous I think) started a blog which, while only containing two articles, has Scott Alexander-tier writing—seriously, people should read it.  Here’s how one article starts:
John Green, beloved YA novelist and AFC Wimbledon supporter, never writes about his parents. If it were not a law of the universe that everything must come from something, you might conjecture that he does not have any. It is proof of his happy upbringing that he—after addressing the prosaic tragedies of human life: falling in love with hot, unattainable people and feeling odd and misshapen and wretched—chose to write about tuberculosis. (His new
book
is available now). Most people write about their parents before getting to tuberculosis. They feel as though they have to. Green did not.
Many of the happiest people I know have the conversational habits of former orphans. I do not mean that they beg for gruel. But they do make very little mention of the company they kept in the years before they turned eighteen. Unhappy people, on the other hand, tend to mention their parents even—
especially
—when it is very inappropriate to do so. All of their bitterness comes bubbling up at a drunken party, just as the music is becoming tolerable and the vodka is beginning to make you like yourself. And you wish the person standing before you now had done anything—vomited, kissed you, kissed the person you had been eyeing all night, used the word “limerence” as if they had invented it—anything but bring up their mother’s utter refusal to know joy.

Some things I read that I enjoyed this year include:
The Mither Mages series by Orson Scott Card.  Also, to a lesser degree, Wyrms, also by Card.  Mither Mages also had a pretty awesome line, spoken by Loki, that was a pretty good articulation of Longtermism:
“If you knew what I know, you’d feel the same. A terrible enemy is poised to rule both worlds with irrevocable power, forever. It would never, never end. Do you understand me? … To keep that enemy from achieving that kind of mastery, that infinite evil, I would let any of you die. I would die myself, if by dying I could be sure of stopping him.”
Forethought’s work
.  Both
Preparing for the Intelligence Explosion
and
Better Futures
pretty radically upended my worldview.  Rare that one group produces multiple papers that totally change your worldview.

The Pledge

My Heart of Hearts

Daniel Greco’s post

about the conflict between Bayesianism and the idea that you should stay away from credences of either 0 or 1.
Orwell’s essay

By far the funniest saga of the year was the continental brouhaha.  It started when I
wrote a post
criticizing continental philosophy for being unclear.  Ellie Anderson wrote a
reply post calling clarity a fetish
and claiming the reason analytic philosophers like clarity is because they believe the correspondence theory of truth.  Daniel Munoz
wrote a reply to the reply giving
the obvious arguments for clarity being good and pointing out the errors in Anderson’s post.  Then, J Edward Hackett
wrote a piece
that, among other things, called Munoz a
self-appointed MIT graduate
.  Munoz dispatched this piece decisively in a
follow-up
.  This led to the
funniest substack comment I saw all year
, from
Ethan Muse
:
On behalf of Professor Hackett, I’d like to challenge you to a formal, moderated debate about whether you actually graduated from MIT.
In the comment thread under Hackett’s post, the best argument you could muster was that there are pictures of you in a cap and gown. Assuming that those pictures havent been digitally manipulated (I consulted with Bart Sibel and he says that Google’s SOTA image classifier has determined there is strong evidence they have been), there are tons of alternative explanations for that data.
Meanwhile, the theory that you graduated is absurd:
(1) - You’re telling me that you were able to graduate in 2014 and 2019 but havent done it again since? That’s very suspicious.
(2) - In the comments on Hackett’s post, you claim that you have now read Adorno. But everybody knows that an MIT graduate would never do that. So were you lying then or are you lying now?
(3) - I’ve constructed a critical-intellectual genealogy that would go way over your head - rest assured, it definitively establishes that there is no way that you graduated from MIT.
If you accept the debate, I will use these and many other arguments to expose the ‘self-appointedness’ of your pretensions to be an MIT alum. Shame!
Hackett also later clarified that when he called Munoz a self-appointed MIT graduate, he had really meant to call him a “self-appointed sanctimonious ass.”  Yet this is even more confusing, because one does not typically appoint oneself a sanctimonious ass.  Does Mr. Hackett know what self-appointed means?
Another highlight
of the continental wars was when someone made fun of me for not knowing linear algebra, which sounds reasonable, but it turned out that they didn’t know linear algebra!  They then went on to claim that I was naive because I thought 1+1=2 is true which, they claimed, it isn’t because of Godel’s theorem—which, as anyone familiar with Godel’s theorem will tell you, is a profound butchering.  Someone else made fun of me for not seeing the profundity of continental philosophy, before claiming that some Chat-GPT generated nonsense paragraph had deep meaning.
Oh, and I guess the most important thing that happened this year: a bunch of you gave money to effective charities.  More than a dozen people took the Giving What We Can pledge.  This will, in expectation, save probably about a hundred lives.  Tens of thousands of dollars were given to the shrimp welfare project.  Potentially billions fewer animals will suffer because of your actions this year.  So thanks guys!  You’re awesome!
